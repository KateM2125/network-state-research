## ABSTRACT

This paper proposes the Network State Classification Framework (NSCF), the first systematic taxonomy for classifying digitally-native communities — variously termed Network States, startup societies, pop-up cities, charter cities, and digital nations — that operate outside traditional nation-state structures while generating measurable economic activity and governance functions. The framework is applied to all 89 entities tracked on the Network State Dashboard (ns.com/dashboard) as at April 2026.

Drawing on established economic taxonomies from the World Bank (income classifications, 217 economies), United Nations (M49 codes, 249 entities; HDI; EGDI; UNCTAD development status), World Economic Forum (Global Competitiveness Index, 140 economies), ISO (3166 country codes), and financial classification systems (GICS, CoinDesk DACS, Messari, Bloomberg), the paper identifies five core assumptions shared by all existing frameworks that Network States violate: territorial contiguity, sovereign recognition, official statistical reporting, involuntary membership, and established legal personhood.

The NSCF addresses this gap with a six-axis classification system: Development Stage (S1-S4), Organisational Type (T1-T6, building on Belarbre's foundational four-category typology), Economic Output (E1-E5, using World Bank income thresholds applied to Gross Community Product), Governance Model (G1-G6), Physical Presence (P0-P5, modelled on UN observer status), and Digital Infrastructure (D1-D5, adapted from UN EGDI). The paper further proposes the Network State Development Index (NSDI), a composite 0-1 score using HDI geometric mean methodology, and the Network State Credit Rating Framework (NSCRF), the Network State Economic Index (NSEI), and the Network State Environmental, Social, and Governance Framework (NS-ESG), each documented in a companion paper as part of a coordinated four-paper research programme.

The framework incorporates non-English-language classification systems from seven language clusters (Chinese, Japanese, Russian, French, Spanish, Arabic, Korean) and identifies four additional classification axes absent from Western frameworks: sovereignty relationship (Russian OKFS), governance function (Chinese platform economy regulation), cyber-physical integration (Japanese Society 5.0), and legal system autonomy (UAE DIFC/ADGM model).

Classification is applied to all 89 tracked entities. Key findings: 75% are at Stage 1 (Startup Society), 82% are at E1 (Subsistence economic class), 30% are virtual-only, and no entity has achieved Stage 4 (diplomatic recognition). Only Prospera (Honduras) has reached Stage 3. The movement is overwhelmingly early-stage, which makes standardised classification urgent — institutions need measurement frameworks before, not after, these entities achieve systemic significance.

This paper is intended as a foundation for formal standards proposals to ISO (via Standards Australia), the United Nations Statistics Division, and relevant international bodies. All data is sourced, cross-validated, and timestamped on the Bitcoin blockchain via OpenTimestamps.

**Keywords:** Network States, economic classification, special economic zones, digital governance, World Bank, United Nations, ISO, WEF, startup societies, charter cities, decentralised governance, GDP alternatives, UNCTAD, NSCF, NSCRF, NSEI, NS-ESG, Gelephu Mindfulness City, NSDI, sovereign recognition

**JEL Codes:** F02 (International Economic Order), H11 (Government Structure and Scope), O33 (Technological Change), P16 (Political Economy)

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

No established global taxonomy accounts for Network States. The World Bank classifies 217 economies by annual GNI (Gross National Income) per capita. The United Nations assigns M49 codes to 249 entities. The WEF (World Economic Forum) benchmarks 140 countries on 98 competitiveness indicators. ISO (International Organization for Standardization) 3166 assigns codes only to entities recognized by the UN. Bloomberg tracks 245 countries and millions of companies through GICS (Global Industry Classification Standard) and sovereign credit frameworks. None of these systems accommodate a cloud-first, geographically distributed, voluntary community with collective economic output, integrated digital currency, and aspirations toward diplomatic recognition.

This brief surveys how the five major institutional classification systems work, identifies the specific gaps that prevent them from capturing Network States, and proposes a rigorous **Network State Classification Framework (NSCF)** and a composite **Network State Development Index (NSDI)** -- an HDI-equivalent for network polities. The framework builds on Eric Belarbre's foundational four-category typology and expands it into a six-axis classification system mappable onto World Bank, UN, and WEF frameworks.

This is designed to be citable. Every claim is sourced.

### 1. WORLD BANK CLASSIFICATION SYSTEM

#### 1.1 Income Groups

The World Bank divides all economies into four income groups, updated annually on 1 July based on annual GNI (Gross National Income) per capita calculated using the Atlas method. The Atlas method averages exchange rates over the current and preceding two years, adjusted for inflation via the SDR (Special Drawing Rights) deflator — the SDR being the IMF's (International Monetary Fund) composite currency basket (USD, EUR, CNY, JPY, GBP) used as an international reserve asset — to dampen short-term currency volatility.

**FY2026 Thresholds (based on 2024 annual GNI per capita, in current USD):**

| Income Group | Annual GNI Per Capita (USD) |
|---|---|
| Low income | <= $1,135 |
| Lower-middle income | $1,136 -- $4,495 |
| Upper-middle income | $4,496 -- $13,935 |
| High income | > $13,935 |

Sources cross-validated: World Bank DataBank (datahelpdesk.worldbank.org, accessed Apr 2026), World Bank Open Data blog (blogs.worldbank.org), and World Development Indicators database. All three sources confirm these thresholds for FY2026.

**Total economies classified:** 217 (189 World Bank member countries + 28 other economies with populations > 30,000).

**Why GNI, not GDP (Gross Domestic Product):** GNI captures annual income earned by a country's residents regardless of where production occurs. GDP measures only domestic production within national borders. For economies with significant remittance flows or foreign investment income, GNI provides a more accurate picture of national income. Both are measured annually in current USD unless otherwise stated.

Source: https://datahelpdesk.worldbank.org/knowledgebase/articles/906519-world-bank-country-and-lending-groups
Source: https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/opendata/understanding-country-income--world-bank-group-income-classifica

#### 1.2 Lending Categories

Separate from income classification, the World Bank uses operational lending categories:

| Category | Criteria | Count (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| **IDA-only** | Annual GNI per capita below $1,315 (current USD) and/or lacking creditworthiness for IBRD | 59 |
| **Blend** | IDA (International Development Association)-eligible but also creditworthy for IBRD (International Bank for Reconstruction and Development) | 19 |
| **IBRD-only** | Creditworthy for IBRD but not IDA-eligible | 67 |

Source: https://datahelpdesk.worldbank.org/knowledgebase/articles/906519-world-bank-country-and-lending-groups

#### 1.3 Regional Groupings

The World Bank uses seven geographic regions for analytical purposes: East Asia and Pacific, Europe and Central Asia, Latin America and the Caribbean, Middle East and North Africa, North America, South Asia, and Sub-Saharan Africa.

Source: https://datatopics.worldbank.org/world-development-indicators/the-world-by-income-and-region.html

#### 1.4 World Bank and Sub-National Zones (SEZs)

The World Bank defines a Special Economic Zone as: "a geographically limited area, usually physically secured (fenced-in); single management or administration; eligibility for benefits based upon physical location within the zone; separate customs area (duty-free benefits) and streamlined procedures."

The Bank classifies SEZ types functionally:

| SEZ Type | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Free Trade Zone (FTZ) | Warehousing, storage, re-export | Jebel Ali Free Zone, Dubai |
| Export Processing Zone (EPZ) | Manufacturing for export | Shenzhen (original form) |
| Enterprise Zone | Distressed urban/rural area revival | UK Enterprise Zones |
| Single Factory EPZ | Individual factory-level incentives | Various developing countries |
| Freeport | Integrated zone with residential | Singapore, Forest City SFZ |
| Specialized Zone | Tech parks, financial zones, logistics | DIFC Dubai, Cyberjaya Malaysia |

#### 1.5 Comparative Definition: SEZ vs Network State

**World Bank definition of a Special Economic Zone (SEZ):**
"A geographically limited area, usually physically secured (fenced-in); single management or administration; eligibility for benefits based upon physical location within the zone; separate customs area (duty-free benefits) and streamlined procedures."
(Source: World Bank, Special Economic Zones: Performance, Lessons Learned, and Implications for Zone Development, 2008)

**Balaji Srinivasan's definition of a Network State:**
"A highly aligned online community with a capacity for collective action that crowdfunds territory around the world and eventually gains diplomatic recognition from pre-existing states."
(Source: Srinivasan, B. (2022). The Network State. thenetworkstate.com)

**Structural comparison:**

| Attribute | SEZ (World Bank) | Network State (Srinivasan) |
|---|---|---|
| Geographic scope | Geographically limited, fenced-in | Geographically distributed, cloud-first |
| Administration | Single management (government-appointed) | Community-governed (founder-led or DAO) |
| Membership basis | Physical location within the zone | Voluntary alignment with shared values |
| Benefits basis | Location-based (tax, customs, regulatory) | Participation-based (access, governance, identity) |
| Customs/trade | Separate customs area, duty-free | No customs borders; crypto-native settlement |
| Creation | Top-down (government decree) | Bottom-up (community formation) |
| Legal status | Recognised sub-national zone | No established legal recognition framework |
| Currency | Host country fiat currency | Protocol-native tokens, BTC, stablecoins |
| Physical presence | Required (the zone IS the geography) | Optional (starts digital, crowdfunds territory) |
| Sovereignty | Host country retains sovereignty | Aspires to diplomatic recognition |

**The key structural difference:** An SEZ unbundles regulation from the host country's default framework but remains within its sovereignty. A Network State unbundles governance itself from geographic territory — membership is voluntary, distributed, and not dependent on physical presence.

**The evolutionary thesis:** SEZs represent governance unbundling within the nation-state framework. Network States represent governance unbundling beyond it. The progression is: Free Trade Zone (1959) → Export Processing Zone (1960s) → Special Economic Zone (1980) → Special Financial Zone (2024) → Charter City (2020) → Network State (2020s). Each generation unbundles more governance functions from the host nation-state.

**Critical observation:** The closest functional analogue to a Network State within the World Bank framework is the "Freeport" — an integrated zone with residential, commercial, and services functions. But even Freeports are government-created, not community-created.

Source: https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/316931512640011812/pdf/P154708-12-07-2017-1512640006382.pdf
Source: https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/WIR2019_CH4.pdf

#### 1.5 What the World Bank Cannot Classify

The World Bank system has no mechanism for:
- An economy without contiguous territory
- An economy whose "residents" are voluntary, globally distributed members
- An economy measured by community product rather than GDP
- An economy without a sovereign government issuing the classification data
- An entity that is not a recognized country or territory with population > 30,000

**Gap for Network States — illustrated across the 89 tracked entities:**

The World Bank system cannot classify any of the 89 Network States tracked on ns.com/dashboard. Specific examples demonstrate why:

| Entity | Why World Bank Cannot Classify | What It Would Need |
|---|---|---|
| **Praxis** (151,068 claimed citizens, $544M raised) | Exceeds 30,000 population threshold and has larger resources than some IDA countries, but lacks sovereign recognition and does not report GNI | Recognised territory + statistical office |
| **Network School** (400+ members, est. $36M GCP) | Per-capita economic output (~$90,000) would place it in E5/high-income, but it is a private membership community inside Malaysia's SFZ | Sovereign status separate from Malaysia |
| **Zuzalu** (20+ derivative pop-ups across Montenegro, Thailand, India, Turkey, etc.) | Distributed across multiple host countries simultaneously — no single geography to assign | Single territory to classify |
| **Plumia** (digital country for nomads) | No physical territory at all — entirely virtual membership | Physical territory to measure |
| **Prospera/Infinita** (Honduras ZEDE) | Closest to classifiable — operates under host country framework — but ZEDE legal status is under constitutional challenge | Stable legal recognition |
| **CityDAO** ($5M treasury, 7,000 members, 40 acres Wyoming) | Has a Wyoming DAO LLC legal structure and physical land, but is governed by token voting, not conventional administration | Government structure recognisable to World Bank |
| **Liberland** (disputed territory, Croatia/Serbia border) | Claims physical territory but no host country recognises it | Recognition by at least one sovereign state |
| **Sealand** (WWII platform, UK waters) | Has existed since 1967, issues passports, has a royal family — but is not recognised by any state | International recognition |

Of the 89 entities: 27 (30%) are virtual-only (P0) — they have no physical presence to classify. 28 (31%) have only temporary/pop-up presence (P1). Even the 34 entities with permanent physical nodes operate under host country jurisdictions rather than their own sovereign authority.

Sources for Praxis data: Praxis official website (praxis.city), CoinDesk reporting on Praxis funding round (2025), ns.com/dashboard (accessed April 2026). Population figure represents self-reported "citizens" (opt-in members) — not residents in the traditional census sense. The $544M figure represents capital raised by the organisation, not GDP or GNI equivalent. Member companies claim a combined valuation of $1.1T — this is self-reported and unaudited.

### 2. UNITED NATIONS CLASSIFICATION SYSTEMS

#### 2.1 UN M49 Standard Country or Area Codes

The UN Statistics Division maintains M49, assigning three-digit numerical codes to countries, territories, and geographic areas for statistical processing.

**Scope:** 249 entities, including:
- 193 UN Member States
- 2 Non-Member Observer States (Holy See, State of Palestine)
- 53 dependencies or territories
- Antarctica
- Other areas reported separately in global datasets

**Hierarchical structure:**
- World (001)
- Continental regions (e.g., Africa = 002, Asia = 142, Europe = 150)
- Sub-regions (e.g., South-Eastern Asia = 035)
- Individual countries and areas

**Key rule:** Assignment to groupings is "for statistical convenience and does not imply any assumption regarding political or other affiliation of countries or territories by the United Nations."

**How non-state entities are handled:**
- Non-Member Observer States (Holy See, Palestine) receive full M49 codes
- Dependent territories (e.g., Hong Kong SAR, Macau SAR) receive codes as "areas"
- Entities without UN recognition do not receive codes (e.g., Taiwan is listed as "Taiwan, Province of China"; Somaliland, Transnistria, and Northern Cyprus are not listed separately)

Source: https://unstats.un.org/unsd/methodology/m49/

#### 2.2 UN Observer Status Framework

The General Assembly may grant observer status to non-member states, intergovernmental organizations, and other entities. This creates a hierarchy of recognition:

| Status | Rights | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Member State** | Full participation and voting | 193 countries |
| **Non-Member Observer State** | Participation in proceedings, no voting | Holy See, State of Palestine |
| **Observer Entity** | Limited participation | International Committee of the Red Cross, Sovereign Military Order of Malta |
| **Intergovernmental Organization Observer** | Issue-specific participation | African Union, EU, Arab League |

**Key precedent for Network States:** Palestine's 2012 upgrade from "non-member observer entity" to "non-member observer state" demonstrates that the UN classification system can evolve to accommodate new forms of political organization. However, the upgrade required a General Assembly vote (138-9-41) and decades of diplomatic engagement.

**Application to the 89 tracked Network States:** Under current UN rules, none of the 89 entities could receive M49 codes. However, several illustrate boundary cases that test the system:

| Entity | UN Classification Challenge |
|---|---|
| **Sealand** (1967, UK) | Has existed for 59 years, issues passports, has a constitutional monarchy — longer than 33 current UN member states have existed. No M49 code. |
| **Liberland** (2015, Croatia/Serbia) | Claims terra nullius, has 700,000+ registered citizens, blockchain-based identity — no state recognises it. |
| **Asgardia** (2016, Global) | Claims 1M+ registered citizens, has a parliament, constitution, and head of state — but exists in space (no territorial basis for M49). |
| **Prospera** (2020, Honduras) | Operates under a host-country framework (ZEDE) — technically classifiable under Honduras's M49 code but with distinct governance, tax, and legal systems. |
| **RNS.ID** (Palau) | Digital identity programme backed by a sovereign state (Palau, M49: 585) — a hybrid where a recognised state sponsors Network State-type infrastructure. |
| **Draper Nation** (Global) | Purely virtual, citizen-governed — no territory, no physical presence, no pathway to M49. |

The 9 Sovereign-Aspirant entities (T5) in the dataset represent the population most likely to eventually seek some form of UN recognition. However, the Montevideo Convention criteria (permanent population, defined territory, government, capacity for international relations) present fundamental barriers for distributed, voluntary, digital-first communities.

Source: https://news.un.org/en/story/2024/04/1148351
Source: https://research.un.org/en/unmembers/observers

#### 2.3 UNCTAD Economic Classifications

UNCTAD uses a development-status classification system:

| Category | Criteria | Count |
|---|---|---|
| **Developed economies** | Northern America, Europe, Israel, Japan, Republic of Korea, Australia, New Zealand | ~40 |
| **Developing economies** | Africa, Latin America & Caribbean, Asia (excl. Israel/Japan/Korea), Oceania (excl. Australia/NZ) | ~150+ |
| **Least Developed Countries (LDCs)** | Annual GNI per capita (current USD) <= $1,088; low Human Assets Index (HAI); high Economic and Environmental Vulnerability Index (EVI) | 44 |
| **Landlocked Developing Countries (LLDCs)** | No territorial access to the sea | 32 |
| **Small Island Developing States (SIDS)** | Remote, high vulnerability to shocks, limited economies of scale | 39 |
| **Transition economies** | Former centrally planned economies | ~18 |

**LDC criteria (three-dimensional):**
1. Income: Annual GNI per capita in current USD (inclusion <= $1,088; graduation >= $1,306). Cross-validated against UNCTAD LDC criteria (unctad.org/topic/least-developed-countries) and UN-OHRLLS (unohrlls.org).
2. Human Assets Index (HAI): health sub-indicators (under-5 mortality, maternal mortality, stunting prevalence) and education sub-indicators (adult literacy, gross secondary enrolment)
3. Economic and Environmental Vulnerability Index (EVI): export concentration, agricultural dependence, remoteness, merchandise export instability, victims of natural disasters, instability of agricultural production

**Review cycle:** Committee for Development Policy reviews the LDC list every three years (next: 2027). Graduation requires meeting thresholds in at least two of three criteria for two consecutive triennial reviews.

Source: https://unctadstat.unctad.org/EN/Classifications.html
Source: https://unctad.org/topic/least-developed-countries/list

#### 2.4 UNCTAD SEZ Classification

UNCTAD provides the most comprehensive institutional taxonomy of special economic zones:

| Zone Type | Primary Function | Typical Size |
|---|---|---|
| Free Trade Zone / Free Zone | Trade facilitation, warehousing, re-export | < 50 hectares |
| Export Processing Zone | Manufacturing for export | 50-500 hectares |
| Enterprise Zone | Urban/regional development | Variable |
| Single Factory EPZ | Individual factory incentives | Single facility |
| Specialized Zone: Tech Park | Science-based industries, R&D | 50-200 hectares |
| Specialized Zone: Financial Services | Offshore finance, fintech | District/building |
| Specialized Zone: Software/Internet | ICT services, coding | Building/campus |
| Specialized Zone: Logistics Park | Supply chain, cargo | Port-adjacent |
| Eco-Industrial Zone | Sustainable development | Variable |
| Freeport | Mixed-use (residential + commercial + industrial) | 500+ hectares |

**Critical observation:** UNCTAD notes that the term "special economic zone" was adopted to be "more encompassing than 'free zones,' which no longer reflected the very wide variety of new zones, especially those oriented towards specific technologies and new services (e.g., health, tourism, security) and sustainable development." This evolution toward functional classification (rather than geographic) is the direction Network State taxonomy should follow.

#### 2.5 UN Human Development Index (HDI)

The HDI is the primary UN measure of human development, covering 193 countries.

**Three dimensions, four indicators:**

| Dimension | Indicator | Min | Max |
|---|---|---|---|
| Health | Life expectancy at birth | 20 years | 85 years |
| Education | Mean years of schooling (adults 25+) | 0 | 15 years |
| Education | Expected years of schooling (children entering school) | 0 | 18 years |
| Standard of living | Annual GNI per capita (PPP — Purchasing Power Parity, in current international dollars, log-adjusted) | $100 | $75,000 |

**Calculation:** Geometric mean of normalised indices for each dimension. The logarithm of income reflects diminishing marginal returns to income. PPP adjusts for price level differences between countries, allowing meaningful comparison of living standards.

Cross-validated: HDI methodology confirmed against UNDP Technical Notes (2025 edition), UNDP Human Development Reports Data Centre, and World Bank PPP conversion factors (data.worldbank.org).

**HDI Classification:**

| Category | HDI Range |
|---|---|
| Very High Human Development | >= 0.800 |
| High Human Development | 0.700 -- 0.799 |
| Medium Human Development | 0.550 -- 0.699 |
| Low Human Development | < 0.550 |

**Why this matters for Network States:** The HDI captures dimensions (health, education, living standards) that are directly relevant to measuring community quality. A Network State equivalent would need analogous dimensions. The methodological choice to use a geometric mean (rather than arithmetic mean) means that poor performance in any single dimension cannot be fully compensated by strong performance in others -- a design principle that should apply to Network States as well.

Source: https://hdr.undp.org/data-center/human-development-index
Source: https://hdr.undp.org/sites/default/files/2025_HDR/HDR25_Technical_Notes.pdf

#### 2.6 UN E-Government Development Index (EGDI)

The EGDI is a composite measure of digital governance capacity across 193 Member States.

**Three components (equally weighted):**
1. Online Service Index (OSI) -- provision of government services online
2. Telecommunication Infrastructure Index (TII) -- digital connectivity
3. Human Capital Index (HCI) -- education and skills

The 2024 edition introduced a new "Digital Government Model Framework" for planning and evaluating digital government initiatives. EGDI is published biennially.

**Relevance to Network States:** The EGDI demonstrates that institutional measurement of digital governance is already established. Network States, which are inherently digital-first, should score highly on digital governance metrics but are excluded from the EGDI because they are not UN member states.

Source: https://publicadministration.un.org/egovkb/en-us/About/Overview/-E-Government-Development-Index
Source: https://desapublications.un.org/sites/default/files/publications/2024-10/Print%20Version%20_E-Government%20Survey%202024%2011_10_2024.pdf

#### 2.7 UN Global Digital Compact (2024)

Adopted at the Summit of the Future on 22 September 2024, the Global Digital Compact is the most recent UN framework for digital governance. It aims to create "an inclusive framework for digital cooperation to ensure an open, free and secure digital future for all." A new UN Office for Digital and Emerging Technologies (ODET) was established on 1 January 2025 to implement it.

**Key relevance:** The GDC addresses digital infrastructure, AI governance, data governance, and digital inclusion -- all directly relevant to Network States -- but focuses exclusively on cooperation between existing nation-states. It does not contemplate digital-native polities as actors.

Source: https://www.un.org/global-digital-compact/en
Source: https://www.un.org/digital-emerging-technologies/global-digital-compact

### 3. WORLD ECONOMIC FORUM CLASSIFICATIONS

#### 3.1 Global Competitiveness Index (GCI) -- Historical Framework

The GCI, last published as a full report in 2019 (discontinued during COVID-19), assessed 140 countries on 98 indicators organized into 12 pillars:

**12 Pillars (GCI 4.0, 2018-2019):**

| Pillar | Category |
|---|---|
| 1. Institutions | Enabling Environment |
| 2. Infrastructure | Enabling Environment |
| 3. ICT adoption | Enabling Environment |
| 4. Macroeconomic stability | Enabling Environment |
| 5. Health | Human Capital |
| 6. Skills | Human Capital |
| 7. Product market | Markets |
| 8. Labor market | Markets |
| 9. Financial system | Markets |
| 10. Market size | Markets |
| 11. Business dynamism | Innovation Ecosystem |
| 12. Innovation capability | Innovation Ecosystem |

**Historical development stages (pre-2018):** The earlier GCI methodology classified economies into three stages based on GDP per capita:

| Stage | Annual GDP Per Capita Threshold (current USD) | Key Drivers |
|---|---|---|
| Factor-driven | < $2,000/year | Basic requirements (institutions, infrastructure, health, education) |
| Efficiency-driven | $3,000 -- $17,000/year | Higher education, market efficiency, technological readiness |
| Innovation-driven | > $17,000/year | Business sophistication, R&D (Research and Development), innovation |

The 2018 GCI 4.0 **abandoned the development stages model** in favor of equal weighting across all 12 pillars (each 8.33%), recognizing that in a globalized digital economy, innovation matters at every development stage.

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Competitiveness_Report
Source: https://www.weforum.org/publications/how-to-end-a-decade-of-lost-productivity-growth/in-full/

#### 3.2 WEF Global Cooperation Barometer (2026)

The GCI's effective successor, the Global Cooperation Barometer, assesses cooperation across five pillars using 41 indicators:

1. Trade and Capital
2. Innovation and Technology
3. Climate and Natural Capital
4. Health and Wellness
5. Peace and Security

The 2026 edition notes that "metrics in which more flexible and smaller arrangements of cooperation can operate -- in data flows, services trade, and select capital flows -- have continued to grow." This finding is directly relevant to Network States, which thrive precisely in data flows and services trade.

Source: https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_The_Global_Cooperation_Barometer_2026.pdf
Source: https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-global-cooperation-barometer-2026/

#### 3.3 WEF Work on DAOs and Decentralized Governance

The WEF has produced two major publications on decentralized governance:

**1. "Decentralized Autonomous Organizations: Beyond the Hype" (2022)**
Co-produced with Wharton BDAP. Classifies DAOs by function and governance model, provides a framework for policymakers to understand the DAO ecosystem.

**2. "Decentralized Autonomous Organization Toolkit" (2023)**
Created with 100+ experts. Provides resources for DAO developers, policymakers, and stakeholders. Recommends that "governments implement policies encouraging the creation and use of blockchain-based commons solutions" and "creating legal frameworks that recognize blockchain-based decision-making processes."

**Key WEF position:** The WEF acknowledges DAOs as "disrupting whole sectors, from finance to social networking to philanthropy, as code-driven, community-governed entities changing the way we work" and that DAOs "manage billions of dollars' worth of assets, engage millions of contributors and operate across industries as diverse as finance and philanthropy."

**Gap:** Neither WEF publication addresses Network States specifically. DAOs are treated as organizational structures, not as proto-polities. The sovereignty dimension is entirely absent.

Source: https://www.weforum.org/publications/decentralized-autonomous-organization-toolkit/
Source: https://www.weforum.org/publications/decentralized-autonomous-organizations-beyond-the-hype/

#### 3.4 WEF Competitiveness in 2030 (2025)

The WEF's 2025 white paper "Global Economic Futures: Competitiveness in 2030" provides forward-looking competitiveness analysis. While focused on nation-states, it acknowledges the growing role of digital infrastructure, platform economics, and cross-border data flows -- all domains where Network States operate.

Source: https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Global_Economic_Futures_Competitiveness_in_2030_2025.pdf

### 4. FINANCIAL DATA TAXONOMIES

#### 4.1 GICS (Global Industry Classification Standard)

Developed in 1999 by S&P Dow Jones Indices and MSCI. The primary system for classifying publicly traded companies.

**Structure:**
- 11 Sectors
- 25 Industry Groups
- 74 Industries
- 163 Sub-Industries

**Coverage:** Over 26,000 publicly traded companies globally. Available on all Bloomberg Professional service desktops.

**Digital asset gap:** GICS was designed for traditional equity markets and has no classification framework for cryptocurrencies, DAOs, or digital-native economic entities. The system classifies by what a company does and where its revenues come from -- concepts that map poorly onto Network States, which are neither companies nor sovereign entities.

Source: https://www.msci.com/our-solutions/indexes/gics
Source: https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/landing/topic/gics/

#### 4.2 CoinDesk Digital Asset Classification Standard (DACS)

Launched December 2021 to fill the gap that GICS cannot address. DACS classifies the top 250 digital assets by market capitalization.

**Structure (three-tiered hierarchy):**
- 7 Sectors
- 26 Industry Groups
- 40 Industries

**Seven Sectors:**
1. Currency
2. Smart Contract Platforms
3. Decentralized Finance (DeFi)
4. Culture & Entertainment
5. Computing
6. Stablecoin
7. Digitization

**Methodology:** Classification is based on the asset's "actual or intended use case or application," determined by reviewing project white papers, social media, on-chain activity, third-party audits, and peer reviews. Monthly reconstitution.

**Relevance to Network States:** DACS classifies tokens, not communities. A Network State with an integrated cryptocurrency would have its token classified under DACS, but the community itself -- its governance, population, territory, and economic output -- remains unclassified.

Source: https://downloads.coindesk.com/cd3/CDI/Digital-Asset-Classification-Standard-Methodology.pdf
Source: https://www.coindesk.com/dacs/

#### 4.3 Bloomberg Entity Classification

**Sovereign coverage:** Bloomberg tracks 245 countries through sovereign debt functions (SOVM), credit profiles (CRPR), and risk data (via partnership with Seerist).

**Crypto integration:** The Bloomberg Terminal covers the top 50 crypto assets with real-time pricing. The Bloomberg Galaxy Crypto Index (BGCI) tracks the largest USD-traded cryptocurrencies by market capitalisation. Following the SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission) / CFTC (Commodity Futures Trading Commission) joint interpretive document of 17 March 2026, which classified 16 major cryptocurrencies as "digital commodities," Bloomberg's crypto coverage has expanded to accommodate this regulatory clarity. Note: SEC/CFTC classification data cross-validated against Federal Register filing (federalregister.gov) and CoinDesk reporting (March 2026).

**Gap:** Bloomberg has no entity category for "Network State," "startup society," or "digital polity." These would need to be classified as either a company (if incorporated) or a fund (if structured as a DAO with treasury). The sovereign dimension is unclassifiable.

### 5. OTHER RELEVANT TAXONOMIES

#### 5.1 ISO 3166 Country Codes

ISO 3166-1 assigns two-letter (alpha-2), three-letter (alpha-3), and three-digit numeric codes to countries and territories.

**Inclusion criteria:** An entity receives an ISO 3166-1 code when its name appears in either of two UN sources: the UN Bulletin on Country Names or the UN Statistical Division's list. This means **UN recognition is a prerequisite for ISO 3166 inclusion.**

**Process for new entities:** The ISO 3166 Maintenance Agency (MA) adds codes based on changes in the UN lists. New codes are assigned when a country changes its name significantly (new alphabetic code) or its territorial boundaries (new numeric code).

**Network State implication:** No Network State can receive an ISO 3166 code without first achieving UN recognition -- Srinivasan's final stage of Network State development. Without an ISO code, a Network State cannot be integrated into global financial systems, trade databases, or statistical frameworks that use ISO 3166 as a reference.

Source: https://www.iso.org/iso-3166-country-codes.html
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_3166-1

#### 5.2 ISIC (International Standard Industrial Classification) Rev. 5

ISIC Rev. 5, endorsed by the UN Statistical Commission in 2023, consists of 463 classes represented by four-digit codes. Implementation by countries begins 2025-2027.

**Key Rev. 5 changes relevant to digital economies:**
- Section J (Information and Communication) split into two new sections: Publishing/Broadcasting/Content and Telecommunications/Computing/IT Services
- 18 new classes for intermediation service activities added across multiple sections
- Better reflection of platform economies, digital services, and tech-enabled activities

**Relevance:** ISIC classifies economic activities, not economic entities. A Network State's members engage in activities classifiable under ISIC (software development, financial services, education, etc.), but the Network State itself -- as a coordinating entity -- has no ISIC class.

Source: https://unstats.un.org/unsd/classifications/Econ/isic
Source: https://unstats.un.org/unsd/classifications/Family/Detail/2095

#### 5.3 OECD Classifications

The OECD does not maintain a formal development taxonomy, but uses several classification frameworks:

**a) DAC List of ODA Recipients:** Countries eligible for Official Development Assistance, classified by World Bank GNI thresholds. Tied aid cannot go to countries above the upper-middle-income threshold for two consecutive years.

**b) R&D Intensity Taxonomy:** Classifies industries by ratio of R&D expenditure to gross value added. Includes non-manufacturing industries, especially services.

**c) BEPS Inclusive Framework:** 140+ jurisdictions committed to combating base erosion and profit shifting. SEZs are scrutinized for harmful tax practices. No country has ignored a Harmful Tax Practices review.

**d) Global Minimum Tax (Pillar Two):** The 15% global minimum tax directly impacts SEZ incentive structures. UNCTAD analysis (2023) warned that Pillar Two may erode the fiscal advantages that make SEZs attractive, forcing a shift from tax-based to services-based zone models.

**Network State implication:** Network States that operate across jurisdictions but are not themselves recognized tax jurisdictions exist in an OECD classification void. Members pay taxes in their respective national jurisdictions. The Network State entity itself may be incorporated as a company (taxable), a foundation (potentially tax-exempt), or a DAO (tax treatment unclear in most jurisdictions).

Source: https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/topics/policy-sub-issues/aid-and-export-credits/cty-class-en.pdf
Source: https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/diaeinf2023d1_en.pdf
Source: https://www.iisd.org/system/files/2024-07/global-minimum-tax-special-economic-zones.pdf

#### 5.4 Estonia's e-Residency: The Precedent

Estonia launched e-Residency in 2014 -- the first government-issued digital identity for global entrepreneurs. Key characteristics:

- Not citizenship; not a travel document; no physical residency rights
- Enables remote access to Estonian business services (company incorporation, banking, digital signatures)
- Recognized by the EU
- Creates a new legal status "not contemplated by statutes in the EU or any MS legislation"
- Raises unresolved questions about double taxation under existing international agreements

**Why this matters:** Estonia's e-Residency is a nation-state creating a digital identity layer for non-residents. Network States are the inverse: digital communities creating physical identity and territorial presence for non-citizens. Estonia proves institutional systems can accommodate novel digital-physical status categories, but only when an existing sovereign initiates them.

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-Residency_of_Estonia
Source: https://www.e-resident.gov.ee

## PART II: THE CLASSIFICATION GAP

### Summary of What Each Framework Can and Cannot Classify

| Framework | Classifies | Cannot Classify (re: Network States) |
|---|---|---|
| **World Bank Income Groups** | 217 economies by GNI per capita | Entities without sovereign data reporting; distributed economies; voluntary membership communities |
| **UN M49** | 249 entities (states + territories) | Non-recognized entities; cloud-first communities; distributed archipelago polities |
| **UNCTAD Development Status** | ~200+ entities by development level | Non-territorial entities; entities outside developed/developing binary |
| **UN HDI** | 193 member states on health/education/living standards | Any entity that is not a recognized state |
| **UN EGDI** | 193 member states on digital governance | Digital-native polities (which would score highly but are excluded by definition) |
| **WEF GCI** | 140 economies on 98 competitiveness indicators | Non-state entities; communities without macroeconomic data |
| **GICS** | 26,000+ public companies by industry | DAOs, foundations, social smart contracts; entities that are not companies |
| **DACS** | 250 digital assets by use case | The community behind the token; governance, territory, population |
| **Bloomberg** | 245 sovereign entities + millions of companies | Network States (no entity category exists) |
| **ISO 3166** | Entities recognized by the UN | Any entity without UN recognition |
| **ISIC Rev. 5** | Economic activities (463 classes) | The coordinating entity itself; "nation-building" as an activity class |
| **OECD BEPS** | Tax jurisdictions | Non-jurisdictional entities; distributed tax structures |

### The Core Problem

Every major global classification system assumes one or more of the following:
1. **Territorial contiguity** -- the entity controls a bounded, continuous geographic area
2. **Sovereign recognition** -- the entity is recognized by other states or the UN
3. **Official data reporting** -- the entity has a statistical office producing GDP, GNI, population, and trade data
4. **Involuntary membership** -- residents are born into or naturalized into the entity, not voluntary subscribers
5. **Legal personhood under existing law** -- the entity is a state, a company, an organization, or an individual

Network States violate all five assumptions. They have distributed territory (archipelago model). They lack sovereign recognition (which is the goal, not the starting point). They do not have national statistical offices. Their membership is voluntary and fluid. And their legal form is novel -- somewhere between a company, a DAO, a social club, and a proto-state.

## PART III: PROPOSED NETWORK STATE CLASSIFICATION FRAMEWORK (NSCF)

### Design Principles

1. **Map onto existing frameworks where possible** -- use World Bank income group logic, HDI methodology, and WEF pillar structure as templates
2. **Add new axes only where existing frameworks have genuine gaps** -- do not create novelty for its own sake
3. **Build on Eric Belarbre's foundational four-category typology** and extend it with measurable criteria
4. **Ensure every classification variable is measurable** -- either directly (on-chain data, membership records) or through standardized surveys
5. **Design for evolution** -- the framework should accommodate entities at every stage from startup society to diplomatically recognized network state

### Axis 3: ECONOMIC OUTPUT (maps onto World Bank income groups)

Instead of GNI per capita (which requires a sovereign statistical office), Network States are classified by Gross Community Product (GCP) per active member.

**GCP components (as defined in the NSEI framework):**

GCP measures the total annual economic output attributable to a Network State's existence and activity. It comprises eight components across three tiers:

**Tier 1 — Direct Economic Activity**
- Direct community revenue (membership fees, services, events, subscriptions)
- Internal transaction volume (member-to-member economic activity, on-chain and off-chain)
- Value of physical assets owned or controlled (real estate, infrastructure, equipment)

**Tier 2 — Business Ecosystem**
- Revenue of businesses founded, incubated, or headquartered within the community (annual aggregate)
- Market capitalisation of businesses founded within the Network State (aggregate of equity and token valuations where available — note: self-reported valuations must be flagged as unaudited)
- Investment capital raised by community-affiliated startups (venture funding, grants, token sales — measured cumulatively and annualised)

**Tier 3 — External Value Creation**
- Contribution to pre-existing host community economy: direct spending by Network State members in the geographic area (rent, food, transport, local services, local employment created, taxes paid to host jurisdiction). This captures the economic impact on the traditional community surrounding the Network State's physical presence. For Network School in Forest City, this includes spending in Gelang Patah, Johor Bahru, and the broader JS-SEZ corridor. Measurement method: member expenditure surveys + local business revenue attribution.
- Knowledge and technology spillovers: open-source contributions, published research, patents filed, educational programmes delivered to non-members, mentoring and advisory provided to host community enterprises

**Tier 4 — Environmental Impact (additive or subtractive)**
Environmental impact modifies the GCP score upward or downward. A Network State that improves its host environment adds value; one that degrades it has value deducted. This mirrors the UN's Inclusive Wealth Index approach and the growing "Beyond GDP" movement.

Positive environmental contributions:
- Carbon offset or reduction attributable to community activity (measured in tonnes CO2e/year)
- Renewable energy generation or procurement (kWh/year)
- Regenerative land use (hectares under active restoration — relevant for regenerative NS like Traditional Dream Factory, OASA, Akiya Collective)
- Waste reduction and circular economy initiatives (tonnes diverted from landfill/year)
- Biodiversity impact (species count, habitat area preserved or restored)

Negative environmental impacts (deducted):
- Carbon emissions from community operations (energy use, travel, construction)
- Land degradation or habitat destruction
- Resource depletion beyond replacement rate
- E-waste generation from digital infrastructure

**Environmental Impact Score (EIS):** Net positive or negative, expressed as a percentage modifier on GCP. A community with +5% EIS would see its effective GCP increased by 5%. A community with -3% EIS would see its effective GCP reduced by 3%. This ensures environmental performance is integrated into economic measurement rather than treated as a separate, ignorable metric.

**Measurement challenges and limitations:**
- Tier 1 data is most readily available (on-chain transactions, published membership fees)
- Tier 2 data depends on self-reporting by businesses — risk of inflation or double-counting
- Tier 3 data requires surveys and estimation — host community contribution is the hardest to measure but among the most important for legitimacy
- Tier 4 data requires environmental auditing capacity that most NS do not yet have
- All tiers should specify whether figures are audited, self-reported, estimated, or modelled
- Double-counting risk: a startup's revenue (Tier 2) may include internal transactions (Tier 1). Methodology must define clear attribution rules to prevent inflation

**Why Tier 3 matters for legitimacy:**
A Network State that extracts value from its host community without contributing will face political opposition, regulatory risk, and reputational damage. Measuring and demonstrating positive contribution to the pre-existing geographic community is essential for long-term sustainability and for host country relationships (directly relevant to DFAT, IRDA, and Austrade engagement). This metric also addresses the criticism that Network States are "digital colonialism" — communities that can demonstrate net positive local economic impact refute this critique with data.

**Why Tier 4 matters for the future:**
The UN's Inclusive Wealth Report, the EU's "Beyond GDP" initiative, and the OECD's Green Growth indicators all point toward environmental integration in economic measurement. A Network State taxonomy that excludes environmental impact would be outdated before publication. Including it positions the NSCF ahead of where institutional measurement is heading — not behind it.

| Economic Class | GCP Per Active Member (Annual) | Analogous World Bank Group | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| **E1: Subsistence** | < $1,135 | Low income | Community is pre-economic; volunteer-driven; no significant revenue or capital formation |
| **E2: Emerging** | $1,136 -- $4,495 | Lower-middle income | Modest membership revenue; early-stage startups; small treasury |
| **E3: Developing** | $4,496 -- $13,935 | Upper-middle income | Significant membership revenue; active capital formation; growing physical assets |
| **E4: Established** | $13,936 -- $90,000 | High income (lower tier) | Substantial economic activity; multiple successful startups; professional services ecosystem |
| **E5: Premium** | > $90,000 | High income (upper tier, Singapore-level) | Exceptional economic output; major capital formation; institutional-grade infrastructure |

**Adjusted GCP:** GCP-Adjusted = GCP-Raw x (1 + EIS), where EIS is the Environmental Impact Score expressed as a decimal (e.g., +0.05 for +5% net positive environmental impact). Classification uses GCP-Adjusted.

**Why use the World Bank thresholds:** Maintaining alignment with World Bank annual income group boundaries (in current USD) enables direct comparison between Network State economic output and national economies. A Network State classified as E4 can be meaningfully compared to upper-middle-income countries. The addition of the Environmental Impact Score ensures that comparison accounts for externalities that GDP/GNI traditionally ignore.

## PART IV: THE NETWORK STATE DEVELOPMENT INDEX (NSDI)

### 4.1 Design

The NSDI is a composite index analogous to the UN HDI. It produces a single score (0.000 to 1.000) that captures the overall development level of a Network State across five dimensions.

**Note:** The NSDI currently uses five dimensions. The Tokenomics axis (K0-K5) is classified separately as it represents monetary infrastructure rather than development maturity. Future versions of the NSDI may incorporate a sixth dimension for monetary sophistication.

**Key methodological decisions (following HDI precedent):**
- Geometric mean of dimension indices (not arithmetic mean) -- ensures that poor performance in one dimension cannot be fully compensated by strong performance in others
- Each dimension index is normalized to 0-1 scale using goalposts (minimum and maximum values)
- The index is designed to be comparable across Network States and, at a high level, to HDI values for nation-states

### 4.2 Five Dimensions

| Dimension | Weight | Indicators | Goalpost (Min) | Goalpost (Max) | Analogous HDI Dimension |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Community Health** | Equal | Member retention rate (12-month); Net Promoter Score; governance participation rate; dispute resolution satisfaction | 0% retention | 95% retention | Health (life expectancy) |
| **Knowledge Capital** | Equal | Mean years of education of members; research outputs per capita; courses/programs offered; guest lecturer caliber index | 0 | 25 years / 50 publications per 100 members | Education |
| **Economic Output** | Equal | GCP per active member (log-adjusted, following HDI methodology) | $100 | $150,000 | Standard of living (GNI per capita) |
| **Digital Maturity** | Equal | Digital Infrastructure level (D1-D5 converted to 0-1); on-chain transparency score; interoperability score | D1 (0.0) | D5 (1.0) | (New -- no HDI analogue) |
| **Sovereignty & Recognition** | Equal | Physical presence level (P0-P5 converted to 0-1); recognition level (0-6 converted to 0-1); number of jurisdictions with legal presence | P0/Recognition 0 | P5/Recognition 6 | (New -- no HDI analogue) |

### 4.3 Calculation

**Step 1:** Calculate dimension index for each dimension:

> Dimension Index = (Actual Value - Minimum) / (Maximum - Minimum)

For Economic Output, use log transformation (following HDI):

> Economic Index = (ln(GCP per member) - ln($100)) / (ln($150,000) - ln($100))

**Step 2:** Calculate NSDI as geometric mean of all five dimension indices:

> NSDI = (Community Health Index x Knowledge Capital Index x Economic Output Index x Digital Maturity Index x Sovereignty Index) ^ (1/5)

### 4.4 Classification Tiers

| Tier | NSDI Range | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Very High Network Development** | >= 0.800 | Full-spectrum network state with strong economy, governance, digital infrastructure, and recognition pathway |
| **High Network Development** | 0.600 -- 0.799 | Mature network with established community, significant economic output, and growing institutional presence |
| **Medium Network Development** | 0.400 -- 0.599 | Developing network with active community but gaps in governance, economy, or physical presence |
| **Low Network Development** | < 0.400 | Early-stage community; primarily online; limited economic output and institutional development |

4. **Three natural clusters emerge:**
   - **Leaders (NSDI >0.35):** ~7 entities with legal frameworks, physical presence, and digital infrastructure. These are the most likely to achieve institutional recognition within 5-10 years.
   - **Emerging (NSDI 0.20-0.35):** ~24 entities strong in 2-3 dimensions with critical gaps. These represent the growth layer — viable if gaps are addressed.

8. **The geometric mean works as designed.** It prevents any single strong dimension from masking fundamental weaknesses. An entity scoring 0.80 on one dimension but 0.05 on another will score lower than one scoring 0.30 across all five. This is the correct design for entities that need balanced development to survive.

#### Caveat on Scoring Methodology and Limitations

**These scores are illustrative estimates (v1.0), not audited assessments.** They are published to demonstrate the NSDI methodology, not to provide definitive ratings of any entity. Users of this data should note the following:

1. **Data limitations:** The majority of scores (65%, n=58) are rated Low confidence (L), meaning they are based on limited publicly available information. Only 1 entity (Prospera) is rated High confidence (H). Scores should be interpreted as order-of-magnitude estimates, not precise measurements.

2. **Conservative bias:** The scoring methodology deliberately scores lower when data is insufficient. This means many entities are likely underscored relative to their actual development. This is a deliberate design choice — it is better to underestimate than to overestimate when building a classification framework.

3. **Self-reported data:** Population figures, membership counts, and economic output for Network States are overwhelmingly self-reported and unaudited. Where self-reported figures are used, they are flagged. Claimed membership (e.g., Praxis: 151,068 "citizens") may diverge significantly from active membership.

4. **Point-in-time assessment:** All scores reflect publicly available information as at April 2026. The Network State landscape evolves rapidly. Entities may have materially changed since scoring.

5. **Geometric mean sensitivity:** The NSDI uses a geometric mean, which means a near-zero score on any single dimension will dramatically reduce the overall index. This is a feature, not a bug — it ensures that critical weaknesses cannot be hidden. However, it means that a small change in a low-scoring dimension (e.g., SR moving from 0.05 to 0.15) can significantly change the overall NSDI.

6. **Invitation to entities:** Network States and startup societies are invited to submit standardised data for re-scoring. A formal data submission template will be published alongside this framework. Entities that provide audited or independently verified data will receive higher confidence ratings.

7. **No commercial or investment implication:** These scores do not constitute investment advice, credit ratings, or endorsements. They are a research exercise in classification methodology. The companion Network State Credit Rating Framework (NSCRF) provides a separate, more detailed assessment for investment-relevant evaluation.

8. **Framework > scores:** The value of this exercise is in the methodology, not in any individual score. If the NSDI framework is adopted and entities begin providing standardised data, scores will improve in accuracy over successive annual iterations — exactly as the UN HDI has improved since its 1990 introduction.

## PART VII.6: SOVEREIGN-RECOGNITION CRITERIA MAPPED AGAINST NSCF AXES (v2.1 ADDITION)

The Montevideo Convention (1933) specifies four juridical criteria for statehood: permanent population, defined territory, government, and capacity to enter into relations. The Convention specifies no economic threshold, but implicit practice under the UN Charter, IMF Articles, and World Bank classification creates a de facto economic floor of approximately $50M GDP and ~10,000 permanent population.

NSCF axes map onto Montevideo criteria as follows:

| Montevideo Criterion | Most relevant NSCF axis | Threshold for recognition-readiness |
|:---------------------|:------------------------|:------------------------------------|
| (a) Permanent population | DM (Demographics) + SR (Sovereign Recognition) | 5,000+ verified active members |
| (b) Defined territory | EO (Economic Output) + SR (Sovereign Recognition) | $100M annual GCP OR $50K GCP per capita |
| (c) Government | KC (Knowledge + Coordination) + CH (Community Health) | S3+ stage (Network Archipelago or higher) |
| (d) Capacity to enter relations | SR (Sovereign Recognition) | At least one host-country bilateral |

Under NSCF v2.1 stage progression, entities at S3 (Network Archipelago) and above have plausible recognition pathways via the SAR / SEZ model (Prospera, Gelephu Mindfulness City) or the associated-state model (hypothetical Network School-Malaysia arrangement). Entities at S1 (Seed Community) or S2 (Event Community) do not yet meet Montevideo criterion (a) and therefore cannot credibly pursue recognition.

**Cross-reference:** See NSEI v1.0 Appendix H for sovereign-state vs NS GDP/GCP per capita comparative tables and the Diplomatic Recognition Readiness Index (DRRI). See NSCRF v1.1 Pillar 5 for host-country recognition risk methodology.

## PART VIII: RESEARCH AGENDA AND LIMITATIONS

### 8.1 Open Questions

1. **Attribution problem:** How do you measure what percentage of a member's economic output is attributable to the Network State vs. their own pre-existing capabilities? The GCP multiplier (currently estimated at 5x membership fees) needs empirical validation.

2. **Active membership definition:** Praxis claims 151,068 "citizens" but active members may be 10,000 or fewer. What constitutes "active" membership? Proposed: logged in to community platform in last 30 days + participated in at least one community activity per quarter.

3. **Legal classification precedent:** No court has ruled on whether a Network State constitutes a "state" under international law. The Montevideo Convention (1933) requires: (a) permanent population, (b) defined territory, (c) government, (d) capacity to enter into relations with other states. Network States at Stage 3+ may meet (a), (b), and (c) but not yet (d).

4. **Tax treatment:** If a Network State collects "membership fees" that fund public goods (education, infrastructure, community services), are these fees analogous to taxes? How should they be classified for member tax deduction purposes?

5. **Double-counting:** A member of both Network School and Praxis would contribute to both entities' GCP. Should the framework address this, or accept it (as the World Bank accepts that MNC revenue appears in multiple countries' GDP)?

### 8.2 Limitations

- **Self-reported data:** Most Network State data is self-reported and unaudited. The NSDI is only as good as the data inputs.
- **Small sample size:** With 117 tracked startup societies and fewer than 10 at Stage 2+, statistical analysis is premature.
- **Survivorship bias:** The framework analyzes existing Network States; dissolved entities (e.g., Vitalia) are excluded, creating survivorship bias.
- **Western/tech bias:** The current Network State ecosystem is overwhelmingly founded by Western technologists and funded by crypto capital. The framework may not capture forms of community organization emerging from non-Western contexts.
- **Rapid evolution:** The Network State landscape is evolving so quickly that any classification may be outdated within months.

### 8.3 Suggested Next Steps

**Phase 1 — Validation (0-6 months)**
1. **Empirical validation:** Conduct member economic surveys at Network School to validate GCP estimates and Tier 1-3 components. Target: minimum 100 respondents across 10+ nationalities.
2. **Governance audit:** Commission independent governance assessments of the top 10 Network States (by NSDI) using the proposed G1-G6 framework.
3. **Complete NSDI scoring:** Calculate NSDI for all 89 tracked entities (currently 8 scored). Publish as open dataset.
4. **Environmental baseline:** Establish Tier 4 (Environmental Impact Score) baseline for 5 entities with physical presence: Network School, Prospera, Traditional Dream Factory, Akiya Collective, Liberstad.
5. **Peer review:** Submit to 2-3 academic journals and seek formal peer review. Target: Journal of International Economic Law, World Development, or Governance.

**Phase 2 — Institutional Engagement (6-18 months)**
6. **Standards Australia engagement:** Present NSCF to Standards Australia as a potential New Work Item Proposal (NWI) for ISO consideration. Identify which ISO Technical Committee is appropriate (TC 68 Financial Services, TC 309 Governance, TC 307 Blockchain, or a new TC).
7. **UN Statistics Division:** Submit NSCF methodology paper to UN Statistics Division for review. Engage through UNCTAD research network and the Global Digital Compact consultation process.
8. **World Bank presentation:** Present to World Bank Development Research Group and request feedback on GCP methodology alignment with GNI/GDP frameworks.
9. **WEF engagement:** Submit for consideration by WEF Global Future Council on the New Agenda for Economic Growth, or the Centre for the Fourth Industrial Revolution.
10. **Multilingual validation:** Engage researchers in China (CASS), Japan (METI/Society 5.0), Korea (KDI), and UAE (DIFC) to validate NSCF applicability to non-Western contexts.

**Phase 3 — Formal Standardisation (18-36 months)**
11. **ISO New Work Item Proposal (NWI):** Submit formal NWI through Standards Australia proposing a new standard for classifying digitally-native economic communities. This requires: the NWI document, justification for the new standard, scope definition, and nomination of project leader.
12. **UN ISIC revision input:** Submit NSCF as input to the next ISIC (International Standard Industrial Classification) revision cycle, proposing new categories for digital-native governance entities.
13. **OECD working paper:** Publish NSCF as an OECD working paper through the Development Centre or the Going Digital project.
14. **Legal opinion:** Commission international law opinion on Network State classification under the Montevideo Convention and UN Charter. Engage The Hague Academy of International Law or similar institution.
15. **Credit rating integration:** Publish NSCRF (companion paper) and pilot ratings for 20+ entities. Engage with DeFi risk rating firms (Exponential, DeFi Safety) for methodology peer review.

**Phase 4 — Institutionalisation (36+ months)**
16. **Establish Network State Statistical Office (NSSO):** Propose a voluntary reporting framework where Network States submit standardised data (membership, GCP, governance metrics, environmental impact) to a central registry — functioning as a distributed statistical office.
17. **NSIC code registry:** Formalise the Network State Interim Code (NSIC) system as a pre-ISO registry, operated independently or through ns.com/dashboard partnership.
18. **Annual classification publication:** Publish an annual "Network State Development Report" analogous to the UNDP Human Development Report, ranking all tracked entities on NSDI with analysis.
19. **Multilingual publication:** Translate core NSCF methodology into UN official languages (Chinese, French, Spanish, Russian, Arabic) plus Korean, Japanese, and German.

## APPENDIX C: DATA VALIDATION METHODOLOGY

All quantitative data in this paper has been validated using the following approach:

**Tier 1 data (institutional thresholds, classifications):**
Cross-validated against a minimum of two primary sources. World Bank thresholds confirmed against DataBank, Open Data blog, and World Development Indicators. UN classifications confirmed against UNSTATS, UNDP Technical Notes, and UNCTAD databases. WEF data confirmed against published reports and archived methodology documents.

**Tier 2 data (Network State metrics):**
Where official data exists (e.g., Praxis funding, CityDAO treasury), cross-validated against the project's official website, at least one third-party news source (CoinDesk, CoinTelegraph, Bloomberg), and the ns.com/dashboard. Population figures for Network States are self-reported opt-in membership counts — NOT census-equivalent data. This distinction is noted wherever such figures appear.

**Tier 3 data (estimates and projections):**
Clearly marked as estimates. Network School GCP and GCP per capita figures are author calculations based on membership fees, estimated member economic output, and Metcalfe's Law network value adjustments. These are illustrative, not audited.

**Timeframes:**
All GNI, GDP, and income figures are annual unless explicitly stated otherwise. Currency is current USD unless otherwise specified (PPP noted where applicable). All data accessed between March-April 2026. Institutional thresholds reference the most recent published values as of that date.

**Limitations:**
Network State data is inherently incomplete. Most Network States do not publish audited financial statements, population counts, or economic output figures. Classification under the NSCF framework relies on publicly available information and may not reflect internal realities. This framework is version 1.0 and will be updated as better data becomes available.
