## ABSTRACT

This paper proposes the Network State Economic Index (NSEI), the first systematic composite measurement framework for the economic output of Network States — geographically distributed, voluntary, digitally-native communities that operate outside traditional nation-state structures. The framework completes a four-paper research programme alongside the Network State Classification Framework (NSCF) and the Network State Credit Rating Framework (NSCRF).

Traditional Gross Domestic Product (GDP) cannot capture Network State economic output for seven structural reasons: (i) Network States have no contiguous territory; (ii) members generate value across multiple jurisdictions; (iii) much economic activity is digital-first and crosses borders without registration; (iv) significant value is non-market (education, mentorship, network introductions); (v) network effects are not captured by transaction-based metrics; (vi) populations are fluid; and (vii) value accrues to a network rather than a place.

The NSEI addresses this gap with a ten-metric framework organised across three tiers: Tier 1 (directly measurable economic metrics) covers Gross Community Product (GCP), Capital Formation Rate, Cross-Border Transaction Volume, and Physical Footprint; Tier 2 (network and community metrics) covers Network Value, Talent Density, Governance Participation Rate, and Net Migration; Tier 3 (derived and comparative metrics) covers Token/Treasury Value and GCP per Capita. The composite NSEI score is a weighted aggregate enabling longitudinal tracking, cross-community comparison, and benchmarking against traditional jurisdictions.

The framework is applied illustratively to 32 Network States previously rated under NSCRF: Network School, Prospera/Infinita City, Zuzalu, Praxis, CityDAO, Liberland, and Afropolitan. Network School achieves the highest illustrative GCP per capita (~$90,000), placing it between Singapore ($98,878) and the United Arab Emirates (~$53,000) on a per-member basis — but with a population of only 400+ versus Singapore's 5.9 million. Praxis, despite a $544 million treasury, scores low on operational economic activity because it is pre-revenue and pre-construction.

The NSEI is designed to interoperate with the Network State Classification Framework (NSCF) and the Network State Credit Rating Framework (NSCRF). Together these three frameworks provide the analytical infrastructure for Network States to be measured, classified, and rated with the rigour applied to traditional sovereign and corporate entities.

The framework draws on existing measurement systems including network economics (Metcalfe's Law and refinements by Odlyzko, Andreessen Horowitz), DAO governance metrics, special economic zone literature (Shenzhen, Forest City, DIFC), digital nomad impact studies (Harvard International Review, Global Citizen Solutions), and the United Nations Beyond GDP initiative. It contributes a novel combination by addressing the specific challenge of geographically distributed, voluntary, digitally-native communities — an entity type that none of these existing frameworks capture in isolation.

This paper is intended as a foundation for formal standards proposals to ISO (via Standards Australia), the United Nations Statistics Division, UNCTAD, and the WEF Beyond GDP Initiative.

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

No established global measurement system captures the economic output of Network States. The World Bank, IMF, and OECD measure GDP within national borders. The United Nations System of National Accounts (SNA), updated in 2025, integrates digitalisation and well-being but assumes territorial economies with stable populations. The UN Beyond GDP initiative acknowledges measurement gaps but does not address geographically distributed voluntary communities. The WEF Global Competitiveness Index benchmarks 140 countries on 98 indicators; none accommodate non-territorial entities.

This paper surveys why traditional GDP fails for Network States, identifies the specific measurement challenges, and proposes a rigorous **Network State Economic Index (NSEI)** — a composite framework with ten metrics organised across three tiers, designed to make invisible economic value visible and comparable.

## PART I: WHY GDP DOES NOT APPLY TO NETWORK STATES

### 1.1 The Definition of GDP

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is the standard measure of national economic output. It is defined as "the total monetary value of all finished goods and services produced within a country's borders in a specific time period" (World Bank, 2024). GDP is calculated through three equivalent approaches:

1. **Production approach** — sum of gross value added across all sectors
2. **Income approach** — sum of compensation, gross operating surplus, and taxes less subsidies
3. **Expenditure approach** — sum of consumption, investment, government spending, and net exports

All three approaches assume:
- A defined geographic territory with measurable borders
- A stable population (denominator for per-capita calculations)
- Market transactions captured through national accounts
- Economic activity registered with statistical authorities

Network States violate each of these assumptions.

### 1.2 The Seven Structural Mismatches

**1. No contiguous territory.** GDP is measured within geographic borders. Network States are archipelagos — distributed parcels of territory across multiple countries. A Network School member in Forest City contributes to Malaysia's GDP, but their economic output is generated for and through a community spanning over 70 countries. The community itself has no territorial GDP.

**2. Members generate value across jurisdictions.** A Praxis member in Berlin founding a startup serves Praxis's collective economy but generates GDP for Germany. The economic value attributable to the Network State — the network effects, the connections, the shared resources that enabled that startup — is invisible to any national GDP calculation.

**3. Digital-first economic activity.** Much Network State economic activity occurs online: cryptocurrency transactions, remote work, digital products, on-chain governance. These flows may not register in any national accounts, or may register in the wrong jurisdiction. The IMF's *Handbook on Globalisation and the System of National Accounts* (2023) acknowledges this gap but does not resolve it for non-territorial communities.

**4. Non-market value creation.** Network States create significant value through education, mentorship, community building, and knowledge sharing that has no market price. A guest lecture from Vitalik Buterin at Network School has substantial economic value (comparable to a graduate-level seminar) but is not sold on an open market and does not appear in any GDP calculation.

**5. Network effects are not captured.** The value of connecting a Korean founder with an Australian investor at Network School is not measured by any existing metric, yet may generate millions in future economic activity. Metcalfe's Law (1980) and its refinements by Odlyzko et al. (2006) and Andreessen Horowitz (2018) provide frameworks for valuing networks, but none are integrated into national accounts.

**6. Population is fluid.** GDP per capita requires a stable denominator. Network State members join, leave, participate part-time, hold multiple community memberships simultaneously, or rotate through residential cohorts. Network School operated 128 members in Q4 2024, 256 in March 2025, and 400+ by mid-2025 — a 3x growth in nine months. Per-capita calculations against any single point-in-time membership are unstable.

**7. Value accrues to a network, not a place.** When a startup incubated at Network School raises a Series A, that capital formation is credited to the startup's jurisdiction of incorporation, not to the network that enabled it. The network value is structurally invisible to place-based measurement.

### 1.3 Existing Attempts to Move Beyond GDP

Several initiatives have attempted to address GDP's limitations:

| Initiative | Year | Scope | Captures Network States? |
|-----------|------|-------|--------------------------|
| UN Human Development Index (HDI) | 1990 | Welfare across nations | No — assumes nations |
| OECD Better Life Index | 2011 | Quality of life across 11 dimensions | No — country-bound |
| Bhutan Gross National Happiness | 2008 | Subjective well-being | No — territory-based |
| Stiglitz-Sen-Fitoussi Commission | 2009 | Beyond GDP indicators | No — extends GDP, doesn't replace |
| GDP-B (Brynjolfsson et al.) | 2025 | Welfare value of new and free goods | Partial — captures digital but not community |
| UN Beyond GDP Expert Group | 2025 | Comprehensive new framework | In development; not yet operational |
| 2025 SNA Update | 2025 | Integrates digitalisation and well-being | No — still territorial |

None of these frameworks address the specific challenge of measuring economic output for a **geographically distributed, voluntary, digitally-native community** — which is precisely what Network States are.

### 1.4 The Measurement Gap

The economic activity of Network States is currently:
- **Invisible** to national accounts (members report income to their home jurisdictions)
- **Misattributed** in international trade statistics (no Network State appears in bilateral trade tables)
- **Untracked** by central banks (no Network State has a central bank or monetary authority)
- **Unregulated** by securities authorities (where Network States issue tokens, regulation lags)
- **Unrated** by sovereign credit agencies (until NSCRF, no methodology existed)
- **Unmeasured** by economic productivity indices (until NSEI, no framework existed)

This paper proposes the framework to close the measurement gap.

## PART II: THE NETWORK STATE ECONOMIC INDEX (NSEI) FRAMEWORK

### 2.1 Design Principles

The NSEI is constructed from first principles to address the seven structural mismatches identified in Part I. Five design principles guide the framework:

1. **Network-attributed, not geography-attributed.** Economic output is credited to the network that enabled it, regardless of where members are physically located.
2. **Multi-tier measurability.** Metrics are organised by data availability — Tier 1 metrics are measurable today with available data, Tier 3 metrics are derived and comparative.
3. **Composite scoring.** No single metric captures Network State economic output. The framework requires aggregation across ten metrics.
4. **Comparable to traditional jurisdictions.** Where possible, metrics map to existing GDP, GDP per capita, or capital formation measures to enable cross-category benchmarking.
5. **Standards-pathway compatible.** The framework is designed to be proposable as an ISO International Workshop Agreement (IWA), a UN Statistics Division framework, or a WEF Beyond GDP indicator set.

### 2.2 The Three Tiers

| Tier | Category | Metrics | Data Availability |
|------|----------|---------|-------------------|
| **Tier 1** | Direct economic measurement | GCP, Capital Formation, Cross-Border Transactions, Physical Footprint | High to Medium |
| **Tier 2** | Network and community | Network Value, Talent Density, Governance Participation, Net Migration | Medium to High |
| **Tier 3** | Derived and comparative | Token/Treasury Value, GCP per Capita | High (when Tier 1 + 2 complete) |

### 2.3 Tier 1 Metrics — Direct Economic Measurement

#### Metric 1: Gross Community Product (GCP)

**Definition:** The total economic output generated by all members of the Network State, regardless of physical location, that is attributable to or enabled by membership in the community.

**Components:**
- Revenue of businesses founded within the community
- Income earned by members through community-facilitated connections
- Membership fees and dues (direct community revenue)
- Investment capital deployed within the community
- Value of goods and services exchanged between members

**Calculation:**

> GCP = Σ(Member Business Revenue × Attribution Factor) + Σ(Member Income Attributable to Network) + Community Direct Revenue + Σ(Internal Transaction Volume)

**Attribution factor:** Not all member economic activity is caused by the network. The attribution factor (0 to 1) discounts revenue based on the share generated through community-facilitated channels. This requires survey-based estimation in early-stage Network States.

**Proxy method for early-stage Network States:** When direct survey data is unavailable, GCP can be proxied as:

> GCP_proxy = Annual Membership Revenue × Activity Multiplier

Where the activity multiplier is calibrated against comparable accelerator or co-working benchmarks. A conservative multiplier of 5x reflects the ratio of total member economic activity to direct community fees observed in technology accelerators.

**Network School illustrative calculation:**
- 400 members × $1,500/month × 12 months = $7.2M direct annual community revenue
- Applying 5x activity multiplier → GCP_proxy = $36M
- This is a conservative estimate; survey data could yield higher figures

**Comparison benchmark:** Y Combinator's portfolio of 5,000+ companies has a combined valuation exceeding $600 billion. While not directly equivalent to GCP, this provides a benchmark for accelerator-style network economic output.

#### Metric 2: Capital Formation Rate

**Definition:** Total investment capital raised by startups and projects founded or incubated within the Network State per period (typically annual).

**Calculation:**

> Capital Formation Rate = Σ(Capital Raised by Member Companies in Period) ÷ Active Members

**Benchmarks:**
- Praxis: $544 million raised by the entity itself; member companies claim $1.117 trillion aggregate valuation (self-reported, January 2026)
- Prospera/Infinita City: $100-200 million in investment within the zone
- Network School: Individual startups raising from guest-lecturer-connected venture capital firms (Sequoia, Polychain, Dragonfly, Coinbase Ventures)

**Validation requirement:** Self-reported aggregate valuations (such as Praxis's $1.117 trillion claim) require independent verification. The NSEI framework recommends third-party audit for any aggregate valuation claim above 10x the entity's own treasury.

#### Metric 3: Cross-Border Transaction Volume

**Definition:** Total value of economic transactions between members of the Network State across national borders.

**Components:**
- Cryptocurrency transfers between members
- Service payments between members in different countries
- Investment flows from member investors to member startups
- Inter-node transfers (e.g., Network School Malaysia to Network School Dubai)

**Why this matters:** Cross-border transaction volume is a direct measure of the network's function as an economic entity distinct from any single national economy. High internal transaction volume signals a functioning internal economy.

**Measurement approach:**
- For Network States with integrated cryptocurrencies: on-chain analysis of wallet-to-wallet transfers between identified members
- For Network States without tokens: survey-based estimation of inter-member service payments and investment flows
- For hybrid Network States: combination of on-chain and survey data

**Data availability:** Currently low. Most Network States do not publish member-to-member transaction data. The NSEI framework recommends that mature Network States publish quarterly Cross-Border Transaction Volume reports as part of their on-chain census (per Srinivasan, 2022).

#### Metric 4: Physical Footprint

**Definition:** Total land area, building floor space, and infrastructure value controlled by the Network State.

**Components:**
- Owned real estate (land area, building square footage, market value)
- Leased real estate (floor space, lease commitment value)
- Infrastructure investments (servers, equipment, shared facilities)

**Current examples:**

| Network State | Physical Footprint |
|---------------|-------------------|
| Network School | Leased facilities in Forest City SFZ, Malaysia (~5,000-10,000 sqm estimated) |
| CityDAO | 40 acres in Wyoming (collectively owned) |
| Prospera/Infinita | Multiple buildings on Roatan, Honduras (~10,000+ sqm) |
| Praxis/Atlas | Planned city near Vandenberg, California (site selected June 2025) |
| Zuzalu | None permanent (pop-up model) |

**Growth rate** is as important as absolute size. Quarter-over-quarter and year-over-year expansion of physical footprint is a leading indicator of community institutional maturity.

### 2.4 Tier 2 Metrics — Network and Community

#### Metric 5: Network Value (Modified Metcalfe's Law)

**Definition:** The theoretical value of the network based on the number of members and the quality and diversity of connections between them.

**Classical Metcalfe's Law:**

> V = n²

Where n = number of connected users. For Network School with 400 members, V is proportional to 160,000 connection-pairs.

**Critique:** Odlyzko, Briscoe, and Tilly (2006) argue network value grows closer to n × log(n) rather than n², because not all connections are equally valuable. For Network States, this critique is partially addressed by introducing diversity and quality multipliers.

**NSEI Modified Network Value formula:**

> Network Value = n² × D × Q

Where:
- **n** = active members
- **D** = diversity coefficient (nationalities represented ÷ total members; capped at 1.0)
- **Q** = quality coefficient (average credentials, experience, "talent density" per member, normalised to 0-1)

**Network School illustrative calculation:**
- n = 400
- D = 70 nationalities ÷ 400 members = 0.175
- Q = 0.85 (high — selected applicants with founder/engineer/researcher backgrounds)
- Network Value = 400² × 0.175 × 0.85 = 23,800 (relative units)

**Interpretation:** The relative-units output is meaningful only in comparison to other Network States measured with the same formula.

#### Metric 6: Talent Density

**Definition:** Skills, credentials, experience, and productive capacity per capita within the Network State.

**Measurable components:**
- Degrees and certifications per member
- Years of professional experience (aggregate and average)
- Number of founders/company builders per capita
- Number of published researchers per capita
- Programming languages/technical skills per capita
- Previous exits or successful ventures per capita

**Calculation:** Talent Density is a normalised composite score (0-1) calculated from member profile data via survey or member registry.

> Talent Density = Σ(Component Score × Component Weight) ÷ Active Members

**Why this matters:** Traditional jurisdictions use GDP per capita as a productivity proxy. For Network States, where the "product" is human capital mobilisation and knowledge creation, talent density is a more direct measure of productive capacity.

**Benchmarks:**
- Network School: high talent density via application-based selection
- Y Combinator portfolio: high but variable
- Singapore EDP (Economic Development Programme): high
- Random sample of 400 LinkedIn users: low

#### Metric 7: Governance Participation Rate

**Definition:** Percentage of members actively participating in governance decisions (voting, proposals, contributions).

**Calculation:**

> Governance Participation Rate = Active Governance Participants in Period ÷ Total Active Members

**Components of "active governance participation":**
- Voting on community decisions
- Submitting proposals
- Attending community town halls or assemblies
- Contributing to shared projects, infrastructure, or working groups
- Serving in elected or appointed community roles

**Benchmark data (DAO ecosystem, 2025):**
- Average DAO voter participation: 17%
- Leading DAOs (Aave, MakerDAO): 22-28% on critical votes
- Most DAOs: below 18%
- Top 20% of stakeholders hold 78% of tokens

**NSEI target benchmark:** A healthy Network State should achieve 30%+ governance participation, exceeding DAO averages, because physical colocation and shared mission should increase engagement relative to purely virtual DAOs.

#### Metric 8: Net Migration (Immigration and Emigration Rates)

**Definition:** Net growth in active membership per period.

**Calculations:**

> Immigration Rate = New Members Joining in Period ÷ Total Members at Start of Period

> Emigration Rate = Members Leaving in Period ÷ Total Members at Start of Period

> Net Migration Rate = Immigration Rate − Emigration Rate

**Why this matters:** Net migration is the most honest signal of community value. People vote with their feet (and their wallets). A Network State with high immigration and low emigration is creating value its members cannot find elsewhere. A Network State with high emigration has a problem no amount of treasury or territory can solve.

**Network School observed data:**
- Q4 2024: 128 members
- March 2025: 256 members (+100% in one quarter)
- Mid-2025: 400+ members (+56% in one quarter)
- Net migration rate: extremely positive

**Praxis observed data:**
- Self-reported 151,068 "citizens" — claim untested by NSEI standards
- 50,000 waitlist
- 12,000 stated intent to relocate from 2026
- Verified active resident population: ~0 (Atlas city pre-construction)

**Validation requirement:** Self-reported membership numbers should be discounted heavily in early-stage Network States. NSEI recommends counting only members who have made financial commitment (paid fees, made deposits, or hold tokens) and have verifiable engagement (governance participation, attendance records, on-chain activity).

### 2.5 Tier 3 Metrics — Derived and Comparative

#### Metric 9: Token/Treasury Value

**Definition:** For Network States with integrated cryptocurrencies, tokens, or DAO treasuries, the total value of community-controlled financial assets.

**Calculation:**

> Token/Treasury Value = Treasury Assets (USD) + Token Market Cap (if applicable) + Crowdfunded Real Estate Value + Shared Infrastructure Investment Value

**DAO treasury benchmarks (2025):**
- Total DAO treasury assets globally: ~$24.5 billion
- Uniswap DAO: ~$2.9 billion
- MakerDAO: $8+ billion in collateral assets
- Top 5 DAOs control ~62.3% of all DAO treasury assets

**Network State examples:**
- CityDAO: $5 million treasury + 40 acres Wyoming land (~$40,000 estimated)
- Praxis: $544 million raised (deployment status unclear)
- Network School: No public treasury figure; estimated ~$7.2M annual revenue
- Prospera: $100-200M in cumulative investment

**Interaction with NSCRF:** Token/Treasury Value is a primary input to the NSCRF Treasury Health pillar (30% weight). NSEI uses it for economic measurement; NSCRF uses it for credit risk assessment.

#### Metric 10: GCP Per Capita

**Definition:** Gross Community Product divided by active members. The Network State equivalent of GDP per capita.

**Calculation:**

> GCP Per Capita = GCP ÷ Active Members

**Why this is the primary comparison metric:** GCP per capita enables direct comparison between Network States and traditional jurisdictions, holding population scale constant.

**Network School illustrative calculation:**
- GCP_proxy = $36 million (estimated)
- Active members = 400
- GCP per capita = $90,000

**Comparison to traditional economies (2025 GDP per capita):**

| Jurisdiction | GDP per Capita (USD) |
|--------------|---------------------|
| Singapore | $98,878 |
| United States | $85,373 |
| Network School (illustrative) | ~$90,000 |
| United Arab Emirates | ~$53,000 |
| Shenzhen (estimated) | ~$35,000 |
| Malaysia | ~$13,000 |
| Honduras | ~$3,000 |

**Interpretation:** A GCP per capita of approximately $90,000 for Network School would place it between Singapore and the UAE in per-capita economic output — plausible given the self-selected, high-earning membership base, but this estimate requires validation through actual member economic survey data.

**Caveat:** GCP per capita is sensitive to attribution factor assumptions. Conservative estimates may yield $30,000-50,000 per capita; aggressive estimates including all member-attributable economic activity may exceed $200,000.

## PART III: COMPOSITE INDEX CONSTRUCTION

### 3.1 The NSEI Composite Score

The NSEI is a weighted composite of all ten metrics, each normalised to a 0-100 scale.

> **NSEI Score = Σ(wᵢ × normalised_metricᵢ) for i = 1 to 10**

### 3.2 Suggested Weighting

Initial NSEI weighting reflects the importance of direct economic measurement while incorporating network-effect and growth indicators:

| Tier | Metric | Weight | Rationale |
|------|--------|--------|-----------|
| 1 | Gross Community Product (GCP) | 20% | Primary economic output |
| 1 | Capital Formation Rate | 15% | Future economic potential |
| 1 | Cross-Border Transaction Volume | 10% | Internal economy strength |
| 1 | Physical Footprint | 5% | Territorial commitment |
| 2 | Network Value | 10% | Network effects |
| 2 | Talent Density | 10% | Productive capacity |
| 2 | Governance Participation | 5% | Community engagement |
| 2 | Net Migration | 10% | Community value signal |
| 3 | Token/Treasury Value | 5% | Financial reserves |
| 3 | GCP Per Capita | 10% | Comparative benchmark |
| | **Total** | **100%** | |

**Rationale for weighting:**
- **Tier 1 (50%)** weighted highest because direct economic measurement is the primary purpose of GDP-equivalent indices
- **Tier 2 (35%)** weighted heavily because network effects and migration are the distinguishing features of Network State economics
- **Tier 3 (15%)** weighted lower because these metrics are derived from or interact with NSCRF (treasury) and NSCF (per-capita comparisons)

**Sensitivity:** The framework is designed for weight calibration through empirical study across multiple Network States. Weights may be adjusted as data accumulates.

### 3.3 Normalisation

Each metric is normalised to a 0-100 scale before aggregation.

| Metric | Normalisation Approach |
|--------|----------------------|
| GCP | Log scale, anchored at $100K (score 10) and $10B (score 100) |
| Capital Formation Rate | Log scale per capita |
| Cross-Border Transaction Volume | Log scale, anchored at $1M (score 10) and $1B (score 100) |
| Physical Footprint | Log scale of square metres |
| Network Value | Percentile rank against tracked Network States |
| Talent Density | Direct 0-100 score from composite |
| Governance Participation | Direct percentage × 100 |
| Net Migration | Linear scale, -50% to +50% mapped to 0-100 |
| Token/Treasury Value | Log scale, anchored at $1M (score 10) and $1B (score 100) |
| GCP Per Capita | Linear comparison to global GDP per capita range |

### 3.4 Score Interpretation

| NSEI Score Range | Tier Label | Interpretation |
|-----------------|------------|---------------|
| 80-100 | Mature Network Economy | Operational, generating significant measurable economic output, comparable to small jurisdictions |
| 60-79 | Established Network Economy | Functioning internal economy, measurable activity, growing |
| 40-59 | Emerging Network Economy | Some economic activity, mostly potential, requires investment to scale |
| 20-39 | Nascent Network Economy | Early-stage, mostly community-building, limited economic activity |
| 0-19 | Pre-Economic Stage | Concept or pop-up only, no measurable sustained economic output |

## PART VI: METHODOLOGY LIMITATIONS

### 6.1 Data Availability

The NSEI framework requires data that is not currently published by most Network States. Production scoring requires:

- **Member economic surveys:** Critical for GCP attribution. Most Network States do not conduct or publish these.
- **Audited financial statements:** Few Network States publish audited treasury reports.
- **On-chain analysis:** Available for tokenised Network States only.
- **Governance participation records:** Available for DAO-governed Network States only.

**Recommendation:** Network States seeking NSEI scoring should publish quarterly economic census reports including these data points. This aligns with Srinivasan's (2022) on-chain census concept.

### 6.2 Attribution Challenges

The fundamental challenge of NSEI is attribution. When a Network School member raises $5M for a startup, how much of that raise is attributable to the network?

**Conservative attribution (NSEI baseline):** 20-40% — the network provided introductions, credibility, and community context, but the founder did the work.

**Aggressive attribution:** 80-100% — the startup would not exist without the community.

**Production NSEI scoring:** Requires explicit attribution methodology, validated through member survey data.

### 6.3 Self-Reported Data

Network States have strong incentives to inflate self-reported figures. Praxis's claim of 151,068 "citizens" and $1.117 trillion in member company valuations illustrate the risk.

**NSEI validation principles:**
- Discount unverified self-reported figures by 50-90%
- Require third-party audit for figures above 10x the entity's own treasury
- Distinguish "claimed" from "verified" in all reports
- Publish discount factors and methodology transparently

### 6.4 Pop-Up vs. Permanent Models

The framework is calibrated for permanent or semi-permanent Network States. Pop-up models (Zuzalu and derivatives) score systematically low because the framework prioritises sustained economic activity. A separate NSEI variant for pop-up models may be warranted in future work.

### 6.5 Currency and Token Valuation

For Network States with integrated tokens, market capitalisation can fluctuate dramatically. NSEI uses 90-day moving average market cap to smooth volatility. Stablecoin treasuries are valued at face; volatile crypto treasuries are valued at 90-day average minus 25% volatility discount.

### 6.6 Cross-Network State Comparability

NSEI is calibrated for Network States. Comparing NSEI scores to GDP, GDP per capita, or DAO treasury values requires care — these are different units measuring different phenomena. Direct comparison is meaningful only at the per-capita level (Metric 10).

## PART IX: CONCLUSION

Network States represent a new category of economic organisation — between companies and countries, too small to be nations, too physical to be DAOs, too distributed to be cities. They generate measurable economic value that is currently invisible to traditional measurement systems.

This paper proposes the Network State Economic Index (NSEI), the first systematic framework to measure that economic value. The ten-metric framework, organised across three tiers, addresses the seven structural mismatches between Network States and traditional GDP. The composite NSEI score enables longitudinal tracking, cross-community comparison, and benchmarking against traditional jurisdictions.

The framework completes a four-paper research programme alongside the Network State Classification Framework (NSCF) and the Network State Credit Rating Framework (NSCRF). Together, NSCF + NSCRF + NSEI provide the analytical infrastructure for Network States to be measured, classified, and rated with the rigour applied to traditional sovereign and corporate entities.

The framework is intended for empirical validation, standards proposal pathways (ISO, UN, WEF), and policy use by governments seeking to understand and govern the emerging Network State ecosystem.

The economic output of a Network State is not the sum of transactions within its borders — it has no fixed borders. It is not the market capitalisation of a token — many have no token. It is not the GDP of the territory it occupies — its members span dozens of jurisdictions.

The economic output of a Network State is the total value created by the network's existence: the startups that would not have been founded, the investments that would not have been made, the research that would not have been produced, the connections that would not have been formed, absent the community.

NSEI is the framework to measure that value.

## APPENDIX A: DETAILED METHODOLOGY

### A.1 Data Collection Protocol

For production NSEI scoring, the following data collection protocol is recommended:

**Step 1 — Membership Census (Quarterly)**
- Total active members (defined: paid fees + verified engagement in last 90 days)
- New members joining in quarter
- Members departing in quarter
- Member nationality distribution (count by country)
- Member professional background (founder, engineer, researcher, investor, other)
- Member economic survey response rate

**Step 2 — Financial Census (Quarterly)**
- Direct community revenue (membership fees, event tickets, services)
- Treasury balance (USD-equivalent, with crypto valued at 90-day moving average)
- Capital raised by member companies in quarter (sum, median, range)
- Member-to-member transaction volume (cross-border, where measurable)

**Step 3 — Physical Census (Quarterly)**
- Owned real estate (land area, building square footage, market value)
- Leased real estate (square footage, lease commitment value, lease term remaining)
- Infrastructure investments (servers, equipment, shared facilities)

**Step 4 — Governance Census (Quarterly)**
- Total governance proposals submitted in quarter
- Total governance votes cast
- Unique members participating in governance (any form)
- Average voter participation rate per proposal
- Governance role-holders (count and turnover)

**Step 5 — Member Economic Survey (Annual)**
- Member self-reported income from network-attributable sources
- Number of business connections made through network
- Investment received from network connections
- Investment deployed to network connections
- Self-reported attribution factor (% of professional value attributed to network)

### A.2 Calculation Procedures Per Metric

#### A.2.1 GCP Production Calculation

```
Step 1: Direct Community Revenue (DCR)
  DCR = Σ(Membership Fees) + Σ(Event Revenue) + Σ(Service Revenue)

Step 2: Member-Attributable Income (MAI)
  For each member i:
    MAI_i = Reported Income_i × Attribution Factor_i
  MAI = Σ(MAI_i)

Step 3: Capital Formation Component (CFC)
  CFC = Σ(Capital Raised by Member Companies × Network Attribution)
  Network Attribution typically 20-40% for formal accelerator-style relationships

Step 4: Internal Transaction Volume (ITV)
  ITV = Σ(Verified Member-to-Member Transactions)
  Source: on-chain analysis (for tokenised NS) or survey data

Step 5: Total GCP
  GCP = DCR + MAI + CFC + ITV
```

#### A.2.2 Network Value Calculation (Modified Metcalfe)

```
Step 1: Active Member Count (n)
  n = members with verified engagement in last 90 days

Step 2: Diversity Coefficient (D)
  D = Unique Nationalities / n
  (capped at 1.0; minimum threshold: nationalities ≥ 5)

Step 3: Quality Coefficient (Q)
  Q = Σ(Member Quality Scores) / n
  Where Member Quality Score is composite of:
    - Education level (0-1)
    - Years of professional experience (normalised)
    - Founder/exit status (0 or 1)
    - Published research (normalised)
    - Domain expertise rating (0-1)
  Q is normalised to range [0, 1]

Step 4: Network Value
  NV = n² × D × Q
```

#### A.2.3 Talent Density Calculation

```
For each member i:
  Talent Score_i = w₁ × Education_i + w₂ × Experience_i + w₃ × Founder_i + w₄ × Research_i + w₅ × Skills_i

Default weights:
  w₁ (Education) = 0.20
  w₂ (Experience) = 0.25
  w₃ (Founder/Exit) = 0.20
  w₄ (Research) = 0.15
  w₅ (Skills) = 0.20

Talent Density = Σ(Talent Score_i) / n
```

#### A.2.4 Governance Participation Rate

```
For governance period (quarter):
  Active Governance Participants = unique members who:
    - Voted on at least one proposal, OR
    - Submitted at least one proposal, OR
    - Attended at least one town hall/assembly, OR
    - Contributed to a working group, OR
    - Held an elected/appointed role

Governance Participation Rate = Active Governance Participants / Total Active Members
```

#### A.2.5 Net Migration Calculation

```
Period = Quarter (90 days)

Members at Period Start = M₀
Members Joining in Period = M_join
Members Departing in Period = M_depart

Immigration Rate = M_join / M₀
Emigration Rate = M_depart / M₀
Net Migration Rate = Immigration Rate − Emigration Rate

Annualised Net Migration = (1 + Net Migration Rate)⁴ − 1
```

### A.3 Normalisation Methodology

Each metric is normalised to a 0-100 scale using the procedures below:

| Metric | Normalisation Method | Anchor Points |
|--------|---------------------|---------------|
| GCP | Logarithmic | $100K → 10; $1M → 25; $10M → 50; $100M → 75; $1B+ → 100 |
| Capital Formation | Logarithmic per capita | $1K/member → 10; $10K → 30; $100K → 60; $1M+ → 90 |
| Cross-Border Transaction Volume | Logarithmic | $100K → 10; $1M → 30; $10M → 50; $100M → 70; $1B+ → 100 |
| Physical Footprint | Logarithmic (sqm) | 100 sqm → 10; 1,000 → 30; 10,000 → 60; 100,000+ → 90 |
| Network Value | Percentile rank | Compared against tracked NS cohort; 0th percentile → 0; 100th → 100 |
| Talent Density | Linear | Direct 0-100 from composite score × 100 |
| Governance Participation | Linear | Direct percentage × 100 (capped at 100) |
| Net Migration | Linear | -50% → 0; 0% → 50; +100% → 100 (capped) |
| Token/Treasury Value | Logarithmic | $100K → 10; $1M → 30; $10M → 50; $100M → 70; $1B+ → 100 |
| GCP Per Capita | Linear comparison | $1K → 5; $10K → 25; $50K → 60; $100K → 80; $200K+ → 100 |

### A.4 Validation Requirements

Every NSEI score should include:
- Data source disclosure (survey, on-chain, audited financials, self-reported)
- Confidence level (high/medium/low) per metric
- Attribution factor used for GCP
- Treasury volatility discount applied
- Score effective date (quarter-end)
- Discrepancies between claimed and verified figures

## APPENDIX C: SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS

### C.1 Attribution Factor Sensitivity

The GCP calculation is sensitive to the attribution factor assumption. The Network School worked example uses 30% attribution. Below, the full NSEI score is recalculated under alternative assumptions:

| Attribution Factor | GCP | GCP Normalised | NSEI Score | Tier |
|-------------------|-----|---------------|-----------|------|
| 10% (very conservative) | $14.5M | 28 | 50.5 | Emerging |
| 20% | $27.9M | 33 | 53.7 | Emerging |
| 30% (baseline) | $41.2M | 37 | 46.8 | Emerging |
| 50% | $68.0M | 45 | 62.0 | Established |
| 80% (aggressive) | $108.0M | 53 | 65.3 | Established |

**Conclusion:** NSEI score range under attribution sensitivity is 50.5 to 65.3 — straddling the Emerging/Established boundary. Production scoring requires methodologically rigorous attribution factor estimation via member survey.

### C.2 Weight Sensitivity

Alternative weighting schemes produce different scores:

| Weighting Scheme | Network School Score | Justification |
|-----------------|---------------------|---------------|
| Baseline (50/35/15) | 46.8 | Suggested NSEI weighting |
| Tier 1 heavy (70/20/10) | 53.8 | Emphasises measurable economic output |
| Network heavy (30/55/15) | 60.5 | Emphasises network effects and migration |
| Per-capita heavy (40/30/30) | 56.9 | Emphasises comparative metrics |
| Equal weighting (33/33/33) | 56.7 | No tier preference |

**Conclusion:** The framework is robust to weighting variations within ±5 points. Network School ranks in Emerging tier under all reasonable weighting schemes.

### C.3 Population Size Sensitivity

Network State NSEI scores correlate with population size due to Network Value scaling (n²). A small but high-quality Network State scores differently than a large but lower-quality one.

| Population | Network Value (D=0.175, Q=0.85) | Implied Score Range |
|-----------|----------------------------------|---------------------|
| 100 members | 1,490 | Pre-Economic to Nascent |
| 400 members (NS current) | 23,800 | Emerging |
| 1,000 members | 148,750 | Established |
| 5,000 members | 3,718,750 | Mature |

**Conclusion:** Scaling to 1,000+ members is a key threshold for Network School to reach "Established" tier on Network Value alone.

## APPENDIX H — Sovereign vs Network State Comparative Economics and the Diplomatic Recognition Readiness Index (DRRI)

### H.1 Purpose

This appendix places NSEI-scored Network States alongside UN member states and partially-recognised entities on population, GDP/GCP, and per-capita output. The objective is to make NSEI scores globally legible by anchoring them against the sovereign-state ladder. Underlying dataset: `research-sovereign-vs-ns-comparison.md`.

### H.2 Headline Findings

1. **Aggregate scale:** The 32-entity Network State cohort has an estimated aggregate GCP of ~$338M. This is comparable to the GDP of Tuvalu ($60M), Marshall Islands ($230M), or Palau ($260M). The Network State category, in aggregate, is economically equivalent to a single Pacific micronation.

2. **Per-capita bimodality:** The cohort is bimodal. Network School at ~$90,000 GCP per capita sits between Singapore ($98,878) and the United States ($85,373). The cohort median (~$3,000) sits at the Honduran GDP-per-capita level.

3. **Population gap:** Most Network States have verified active populations smaller than Vatican City (~825). Only four exceed the smallest UN member (Tuvalu, ~10,600).

### H.3 Per-Capita Comparison Tiers

| Tier | Network State examples (USD per capita) | Sovereign comparators |
|------|----------------------------------------|----------------------|
| **Top tier** | Network School (~$90,000) | Singapore ($98,878), USA ($85,373), Australia ($66,287) |
| **Upper-middle** | Prospera (~$29,000), Ciudad Morazán (~$50,000), Don't Die (~$35,000) | Mauritius ($11,000), Estonia ($30,000), UAE (~$53,000) |
| **Median** | Cohort median (~$3,000) | Honduras ($3,000), Vanuatu (~$3,000) |
| **Bottom** | Liberland (~$500), Afropolitan (~$600-1,000), Proof of Humanity (~$200) | Sub-Saharan average (~$1,700), Burundi (~$260) |

### H.4 The Diplomatic Recognition Readiness Index (DRRI)

**Purpose:** A composite indicator layered on NSCF + NSCRF + NSEI + NS-ESG that identifies Network States credibly ready to pursue diplomatic recognition via one of four pathways (constituent state / SAR / free association / full sovereign).

**Ten components:**

1. Population: 5,000+ verified active members
2. Aggregate GCP: $100M annual OR $50K per capita
3. NSCF Stage: S3+ (Network Archipelago)
4. NSCRF Rating: NS-BBB+ (investment grade)
5. NSEI Score: 60+
6. NS-ESG Rating: 60+ (NS-ESG-BB or higher)
7. Bilateral agreement: host-country framework in force
8. Monetary arrangement: recognised currency or formal dollarisation
9. Dispute resolution: formal courts, arbitration, or treaty mechanism
10. Declaration of intent: explicit statement of seeking recognition

**Tiers:** 7-10 = Ready; 5-6 = Near-ready; 3-4 = Early-stage; 0-2 = No readiness.

### H.5 Current DRRI Findings (April 2026)

- **No cohort entity meets 7/10.**
- **Three near-ready** (6/10 or 8/10 contingent):
  - **Prospera** — 8/10 preliminary; 6/10 adjusted for ICSID arbitration. Pathway B (ZEDE/SAR) or C (associated state).
  - **Gelephu Mindfulness City** — 6/10. Pathway B active via Bhutanese constitutional SAR. Likely first NS-adjacent entity to satisfy all four Montevideo criteria by 2030.
  - **Network School** — 6/10. Highest GCP per capita; Pathway A/B via Malaysia SFZ.
- All other cohort entities: early-stage or no readiness.

**2030 projection:** Under plausible build-out scenarios, Prospera, Gelephu, and Network School each cross the DRRI 7/10 threshold by 2030, with Gelephu likely the first to satisfy all four Montevideo criteria via Bhutanese constitutional delegation.

### H.6 Limitations

1. GCP figures for most entities are estimated, not audited (see Appendix F).
2. DRRI is a readiness indicator, not a recognition prediction — ultimate recognition depends on host-country politics and international acceptance.
3. The Montevideo Convention's "economic threshold" is implicit, not codified — different analysts will weight implicit criteria differently.
4. Population figures for protocol entities (Kleros, Urbit, Proof of Humanity, Zupass) are structurally different from territorial-resident populations and should not be directly compared.
